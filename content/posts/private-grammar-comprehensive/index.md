## Summary

Pupils at private and grammar schools do better than pupils at comprehensives: better A levels, more university places, higher pay. But these schools choose their pupils, by fees or by an entrance test, and the pupils they choose would often have done well anywhere. This article asks how much of the advantage is the school and how much is the intake, and whether, at today's fees, private school pays for itself in lifetime earnings.

It uses public data from the Department for Education (DfE), the University of Cambridge and the Office for National Statistics (ONS); 25 published studies (303 of the 312 figures taken from them were checked word for word against the papers); current tax rules and house prices; 2025/26 national average fees and 2026/27 fees for the top schools. It also profiles the top 20 private schools for boys, the top 20 for girls and the top 30 grammar schools.

**The answer: on the best current evidence, private day school from 11 to 18 probably does not pay for itself in lifetime earnings for a typical private pupil. Counting the extra pay after tax against the fees, both in today's money, a boy ends up about £53k worse off and a girl about £68k worse off; put another way, the fees earn a return of only 1.8% a year for a boy and 1.3% for a girl after inflation, less than a mortgage costs. The evidence is uncertain enough that a typical boy could just break even (+£7k); a typical girl would still lose (−£18k). At the top 20 schools, whose fees are higher, the loss is larger: about £103k for a boy and £115k for a girl.**

- **Grammar schools now match private schools on most raw results.** By 19, 88.8% of grammar sixth-formers and 86.9% of private sixth-formers had gone to university, against 77.2% at other state schools and colleges. For every A-level student they have, grammar sixth forms now get 10%-24% more pupils into Cambridge than private sixth forms (Figure 1).
- **Much of the pay gap comes from who gets in.** At 30, the middle earner from a private school earns 1.45 times as much as the middle earner from a state school (excluding pupils on free school meals), and at the top the gap is wider still. Comparing people with similar family backgrounds removes 37% to 71% of the private-school pay gap in different studies. For the most recent generation studied (born 1989-90) it leaves an advantage of about 17%: chance alone puts the true figure between 5% and 31%, and it may be lower still, because that study could not allow for early test scores. For grammar schools, studies of children who just passed or just missed the 11-plus find about no pay difference.
- **The fees set a high bar.** Day fees from 11 to 18 cost £149k in today's money. To earn that back, a typical private pupil would need to earn about 27% more for life (boys) or 33% more (girls), well above the central estimate of 17%.
- **It is the timing, not the size, of the gain that loses money.** Over a whole career the same boy earns £291k more after tax, against £165k of fees. But the fees are paid now and the extra pay comes over the next 50 years, and money now is worth more than money later.
- **Pupils at top private schools beat their GCSE prediction at A level; at top grammars, barely.** Pupils at the top private schools get about +0.21 of a grade more per A level than their GCSEs predicted; at the top grammars, +0.05. Within the top private schools, higher fees do not go with more of this added value.
- **The cheaper routes cost far less.** Paying for a house in a good school's catchment costs £65k in London, including about £29.9k of stamp duty, if the school is the only reason to move; a grammar school costs only the tutoring.

These are averages and say nothing about a particular child or school. The evidence on pay comes from people who were at school between the 1960s and 2000s, and some of the remaining advantage may still be who gets in rather than what the school does. Pay is only one reason families choose a school.

## 1. What is compared, and how

### 1.1 Three kinds of comparison

Every number below is one of three kinds:

- **Raw.** The plain difference between pupils at each kind of school. It mixes what the school does with who it admits.
- **Allowing for intake.** Compares pupils who were similar *before* they started at the school: similar family background and, where measured, similar test scores at 7, 10 or 11. This is the "allowing for the entrance filter" comparison. It still cannot prove that the school caused the difference: families who choose private school may differ in ways no survey records.
- **Allowing for later results.** Also compares pupils with the same GCSEs, A levels, degree or university. Because those are partly the school's doing, this removes some of the school's own effect; it shows what is left beyond exam results.

"Comprehensive" means a non-selective state school outside the areas that still have grammar schools. Non-selective schools inside those areas (the modern secondary moderns) and the 24 schools that select some of their places by ability ("partially selective", such as Dame Alice Owen's and the Watford grammars) are counted separately. England only.

### 1.2 How to read the numbers

- **Percentile.** Rank out of 100. A child "at the 64th percentile" of earnings earns more than 64 in every 100 people of the same sex and age. The 50th percentile is the middle (the median).
- **Today's money (present value).** Money paid or received in future is worth less than money now, because money now could be saved or used to pay off a mortgage. Future amounts are therefore marked down by a set rate a year (the *discount rate*) to give their value today. This article uses 3.5% a year for the first 30 years and 3.0% after, the rule HM Treasury tells government departments to use (the "Green Book"), and also 2.1%, roughly the cost of a mortgage after inflation. All amounts are in 2025/26 prices, so inflation is already taken out.
- **Net gain (net present value).** The value today of the extra lifetime pay after tax, minus the value today of the fees. Below zero means the fees do not pay for themselves.
- **Return on the fees.** The yearly interest rate the fees would be earning if the extra pay were thought of as interest on them. A school "pays" if this beats the discount rate.
- **Range (95% interval).** The range within which the true figure very probably lies, given how many people a study observed. It allows for the luck of who happened to be in the study, not for flaws in the comparison itself.
- **Value added.** How much better pupils do at A level than their GCSE grades predicted, in grades per A level. Positive means pupils do better than similar pupils elsewhere.
- **Progress 8.** The same idea for GCSEs, measured against national tests taken at 11 (KS2).

## 2. University and A levels

### 2.1 University

By 19, 86.9% of private-school A-level students had gone to university, 88.8% of grammar sixth-formers and 77.2% of those at other state schools and colleges (DfE, pupils aged 15 in 2020/21). Private sixth-formers are more likely to go to universities with high entry grades (58.6%, against 50.2% from grammars and 24.2% from other state schools). These rates have barely moved in 15 years of data. They describe the school attended at 17, so "grammar" here means grammar-school sixth forms, which also take pupils at 16.

In 2013, private sixth-formers were the most likely to get into Cambridge for every A-level student they had. By 2023 grammar sixth-formers were 10%-24% more likely (26.2 against 21.1-23.8 per 1,000); the lines crossed between 2019 and 2022, depending on which private pupils are counted. Of those who applied in 2025, 31.7% of grammar applicants got an offer, against 25.9% of private applicants and 22.7% of applicants from comprehensives. None of this allows for who applies, so it does not show what the schools themselves do; it shows that grammar sixth forms have overtaken private ones at Cambridge.

[![Range chart of A-level value added by kind of school, 2024/25: private median +0.21, grammar +0.01, partially selective −0.04, comprehensive −0.02, non-selective in grammar areas −0.14.](fig-va-sectors.svg)](fig-va-sectors.svg)

*Figure 2. A-level value added by school, 2024/25: how many grades per A level pupils gained above what their GCSEs predicted. The line spans the middle 80% of schools, the bar the middle 50%, and the tick marks the middle school. (click for full size)*

On raw results, private and grammar sixth forms are close: 42.9 and 41.6 points per A level (both about a B+), against 33.9 at comprehensives. Value added separates them. Private sixth-formers gain +0.20 of a grade per A level above their GCSE prediction on average; grammar sixth-formers +0.01; comprehensive pupils −0.02; pupils at non-selective schools in grammar areas −0.12. (These averages weight each school by its number of A levels; Figure 2 shows the middle school instead.)

Two cautions. Value added covers the sixth form only, so it says nothing about ages 11-16. And many private schools use IGCSEs, which DfE does not count, so some private pupils have no GCSE baseline and are left out (fewer than 70% of A levels are covered at 53 private schools, but at only 1 of the top schools in §3).

For ages 11-16, Progress 8 compares GCSEs with tests at 11. Grammar schools score +0.67, comprehensives 0.00 and non-selective schools in grammar areas −0.19. Private schools have no Progress 8. Grammar schools select on more than the tests at 11 (their own entrance test, tutoring, motivation), so +0.67 overstates what they add. A study of twins that also allowed for a genetic score found the grammar-school GCSE advantage shrank to 0.03 of a standard deviation, close to nothing (Smith-Woolley et al. 2018).

## 3. The top schools

### 3.1 How the lists were chosen

Schools are ranked by A-level points per entry over 2023/24 and 2024/25 (A levels plus the IB and similar qualifications), counting only schools with at least 40 students in each year. Sixth-form-only colleges are left out, and so are international colleges (private schools where fewer than half of A levels have an English GCSE baseline; this removes Concord College).

There are two private lists, because a family's choice depends on the child:
- **Top 20 open to boys:** boys' schools and mixed schools.
- **Top 20 open to girls:** girls' schools and mixed schools that a girl starting at 11 or 13 in September 2027 can attend, checked against each school's admissions page. Schools that for 2027 take girls only into the sixth form (King's College School, Magdalen College School, Winchester College) are on the boys' list only.

A mixed school can appear on both lists. DfE counts a school as mixed if it takes girls at any age, so only 23 schools in the whole ranking count as boys' schools; but 10 of the 20 schools on the boys' list admit only boys at 11 or 13.

Value added and Cambridge places are shown beside the ranking but not used for it. Cambridge places are pooled over the 2022-2025 admissions rounds and divided by the school's sixth-form leavers in the same years.

## 4. Pay: raw and allowing for intake

### 4.1 The raw gap is widest at the top

[![Line chart of private pupils' pay at 30 relative to state pupils' (not on free school meals), at each percentile: 1.25 at the 10th, 1.45 at the 50th, 1.61 at the 90th, 2.12 at the 99th; for pupils with the same GCSE results the ratio is 1.18-1.39.](fig-ons-ratio.svg)](fig-ons-ratio.svg)

*Figure 6. Pay at 30 (from tax records: ONS 2022, people aged 30 in 2017-2019) of private-school pupils relative to state pupils not on free school meals, rank by rank. Grey band: the same comparison for pupils with the same GCSE results (averages). (click for full size)*

At 30, the middle private-school earner earns 1.45 times as much as the middle state-school earner. The gap is about the same from the 25th to the 75th percentile (1.48 and 1.48 times) and widens only in the top quarter: 1.61 times at the 90th percentile and 2.12 times at the 99th (£181k against £85k a year). Among pupils with the same GCSE results the gap in average pay is 1.18-1.39 times; but that comparison holds GCSEs fixed and so removes part of what the school does. ONS does not split state schools into grammar and comprehensive.

### 4.2 What is left once intake is allowed for

[![Chart of pay advantages by study, raw (hollow) and allowing for background and early test scores (filled; the 1989-90 group for background only): born 1958 7% (men and women; men against comprehensives 25%); born 1970, men 20% at 34 and 35% at 42, women 28% and 21%; born 1989-90 17% at 25; graduates 10% men and 5% women; grammar 9%, 7%, −3% and 8%.](fig-evidence.svg)](fig-evidence.svg)

*Figure 7. The private-school (blue) and grammar-school (orange) pay advantage in each study: raw (hollow circle) and after comparing pupils with similar family backgrounds and early test scores (filled, with the range where the study gives one). Graduates: also the same A levels, degree and university. (click for full size)*

Every study finds the same pattern. Comparing like with like removes a large part of the raw private-school pay gap: between 37% and 71% of it in the studies of people born in 1958, 1970 and 1989-90 (measured on the log scale economists use to compare pay gaps; Figure 7 shows most of these comparisons). For people born in 1970, the private-school advantage after allowing for background and early test scores was 20% for men at 34 and 35% at 42, and 28% for women at 34 and 21% at 42 (Green, Machin, Murphy and Zhu 2012; Green, Henseke and Vignoles 2017). For men born in 1958 it was only 8% compared with all state schools, but 25% compared with comprehensives (Dearden, Ferri and Meghir 2002). The working-paper version of the 2012 study does not say at what age school type was recorded, and a later paper co-authored by Green describes it as private *primary* school; if so, its test scores at 10 and 11 may already reflect the school.

The most recent estimate is for people born in 1989-90 who were at private school at 13: 17% higher weekly pay at 25, comparing people with similar family backgrounds but without early test scores (Green, Anders, Henderson and Henseke 2020). For people born in 1970, adding early test scores barely changed the answer. But for children born in 2000-02, adding gender, ethnicity and test scores at age 3 moves the private-school GCSE advantage from +0.05 to −0.12 of a grade (Anders et al. 2024; neither figure can be told apart from chance). So the 17% for 1989-90, which cannot allow for early test scores, may overstate the private-school effect.

Three findings matter most for children starting school now:

- **The exam-results route may have closed.** For children born in 2000-02 the private-school GCSE advantage is +0.05 of a grade once family background is allowed for, about nothing (Anders et al. 2024). For people born in 1970, about half of the rise in the pay advantage came through better qualifications.
- **The sixth-form route is still open.** Among A-level students born in 1989-90, those at private sixth forms were 9 percentage points more likely to go to university than state pupils with the same GCSEs and family background, and those who went were 10 percentage points more likely to go to a Russell Group university (Henderson et al. 2020). This study's value-added figures point the same way.
- **What is left after qualifications is smaller.** Among people who graduated in 2006/07, private-school graduates earned 9.5% more (men) and 4.7% more (women) three and a half years later than state-school graduates with the same A levels, degree class, university and subject (Crawford and Vignoles 2014).

For grammar schools, the strongest evidence compares children who just passed the 11-plus with children who just missed it, since they are almost identical except for the school they went to. These studies find about no pay difference: −3% for men and +8% for women (too uncertain to tell from zero) in hourly pay estimated from occupation, in Aberdeen in the 1950s (Clark and Del Bono 2016; −6% and +17% in yearly income, which also reflects hours worked), and +7% with a very wide range for people born in 1958 (Pastore and Jones 2023). Studies that instead compare pupils with similar background and test scores find more (9% for men born in 1958; Dearden, Ferri and Meghir 2002). But the same method finds a "grammar-school effect" on test scores taken *before* children reached grammar school, which is impossible, so it overstates (Manning and Pischke 2006).

The pass-mark studies compare grammar pupils with secondary moderns; Dearden et al. compare them with comprehensives in the mixed system of 1974. No study has yet measured pay for anyone who sat the 11-plus after the 1990s, although their tax records now exist. For a family in a grammar-school area, the alternative today is the local non-selective school (value added −0.12, Progress 8 −0.19), much like the old secondary modern. For a family in a comprehensive area, the pass-mark studies fit less well.

### 4.3 Three scenarios for today's pupils

The cost-benefit calculation uses three scenarios for the pay advantage, chosen to cover the range of the evidence rather than to bet on one number:

| Scenario | Private, 11-18 | Grammar, 11-18 | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | rising from 17% at 25 to 35% (men) or 28% (women) | 9.0% (men), 7.7% (women) | private: people born in 1970, unchanged (for women, the higher of their figures at 34 and 42); grammar: men the less reliable like-for-like method, women the pass-mark method (very uncertain) |
| Central | 17% at every age | 0 | private: people born in 1989-90 at 25; grammar: pass-mark studies |
| Low | 9.5% (men), 4.7% (women) | 0 | only what is left after qualifications |

The central figure could lie anywhere from 5% to 31%, and results are given at both ends of that range. The central scenario assumes that the advantage's growth with age, seen for people born in 1970, is cancelled out by today's smaller exam advantage. The low scenario is not a floor: a pay advantage of zero cannot be ruled out.

For private sixth form only (16-18), the range is 0 to 6.3%. The central 1.7% counts only one route (more pupils going to university) and is only a rough pricing of it; the high figure is the whole education route for people born in 1989-90.

## 5. Does private school pay?

### 5.1 How the calculation works

For a child at a given percentile, pay at each age from 22 to 67 comes from the 2025 national earnings survey (ASHE), for men or women and for all employees, full- and part-time. Each year is weighted by the chance of being in work: 89.5% for male and 85.9% for female graduates (DfE). Pay grows 1.7% a year faster than prices. The pay advantage raises yearly pay by the scenario's percentage, and the gain is counted after income tax, National Insurance and student-loan repayments, with tax thresholds frozen until 2031 as announced.

Fees are the national average for day schools including VAT (ISC census 2026, 2025/26 fees): £149k in today's money for ages 11-18. Scaled by ISC's regional figures, that is about £184k in London and £109k in the North West. Section 6 does the same calculation with the fees of the top schools. Fees are assumed to stay level after inflation, and extras (lunches, trips, uniform) are left out; both make private school look better than it is. Bursaries and sibling discounts are also left out, which makes it look worse (a 10% discount is one of the bars in Figure 9).

Where the child would otherwise sit in the earnings ranking matters, because a percentage pay rise is worth more to a high earner. A typical private pupil would, at a state school, earn at about the 64th percentile. This comes from taking the middle private pupil's pay at 30, removing the pay advantage, and seeing where that falls among state pupils (ONS).

The calculation makes these further assumptions:
- The child would hold the same place in the earnings ranking whichever school they went to.
- ONS does not split this by sex, so in the central scenario boys and girls get the same place (it differs slightly in the other scenarios only because the pay advantage does). It is a place among everyone with any pay from employment at 30, used as a place among employees in ASHE, so it is approximate.
- The school does not change how many hours someone works or whether they work at all.
- The child goes to university either way, so takes out the same student loan (though repays more if paid more).
- Graduates' employment rate for ages 16-64 also holds from 64 to 67.

The private-school figures describe, roughly, children whose parents actually chose private school. A family on the fence (for example one priced in or out by VAT) might gain more or less; the evidence cannot say which. The grammar pass-mark figures, by contrast, already describe children close to the cut-off.

### 5.2 The pay advantage needed to break even

[![Chart of the lifetime pay advantage needed to repay 11-18 day fees, by the child's earnings rank, for men and women, at 3.5% and 2.1%, with the three scenarios: at the median, 31% (men) and 38% (women) at 3.5%; the central scenario is 17%.](fig-breakeven.svg)](fig-breakeven.svg)

*Figure 8. The lifetime pay advantage needed to repay 11-18 day fees (black lines), against the scenarios for today's pupils (blue). (click for full size)*

To repay the fees, a child at the middle of the earnings ranking needs to earn 31% more for life (boys) or 38% more (girls) at a 3.5% discount rate, or 21% and 27% at 2.1%. At the typical private pupil's place the bar is about 27% for boys and 33% for girls; at the 90th percentile it falls to 15% and 23%. The bar is higher for girls because women are more often out of work or part-time, so a percentage pay rise adds up to less over a career.

### 5.4 What changes the answer

[![Chart showing how the net gain for a typical boy and girl changes as each assumption changes: the pay-advantage scenario matters most; then, for boys, the child's earnings rank and for girls the discount rate; then fees and the rest.](fig-tornado.svg)](fig-tornado.svg)

For a typical boy the answer depends first on which pay advantage applies to today's pupils and on where he would otherwise sit in the earnings ranking, then on the discount rate and the fees. He comes out ahead only:
- if the advantage is as large as for people born in 1970 (+£7k);
- if he would be a top-10% earner anyway (+£18k);
- or at the top of the central range (+£7k).

A 2.1% discount rate brings him close to breaking even (−£12k). The fees of the top 20 schools for boys take him to −£103k (as in §6), and a 10% bursary or sibling discount lifts him to −£38k. For a typical girl, the scenario and the discount rate matter most, and no single change on its own makes the fees pay.

## 7. The cheaper alternatives

### 7.1 Moving house

Buying a home in a good state school's catchment means paying more for the house. Most of that extra price comes back when the house is sold, so the cost is not the extra price itself but the cost of holding it: the interest that money could otherwise earn, the stamp duty, and the costs of buying and selling. House prices are those paid by existing homeowners who moved in July 2026 (UK House Price Index): £357,496 in England, £476,549 in the South East and £693,923 in London. The catchment premium is assumed to be 10-20% of the price, a range that spans the published estimates (from studies of small differences in school quality to estimates for homes near the best state schools), and the house is held for seven years.

| | England | South East | London |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extra price for the catchment (15%) | £54k | £71k | £104k |
| Stamp duty on the whole new home (with the 15% premium) | £10.6k | £17.4k | £29.9k |
| of which caused by the premium | £2.7k | £3.6k | £5.2k |
| Cost in today's money, family moving anyway (premium 10-20%) | £9k-£17k | £12k-£23k | £17k-£34k |
| Cost in today's money, school the only reason to move (premium 10-20%) | £25k-£34k | £36k-£48k | £57k-£73k |

*Table 8. The cost of a catchment premium held for seven years, at 3.5%. A family moving anyway pays only the extra stamp duty on the premium and the cost of holding it. A family moving only for the school also pays the stamp duty on the whole new home, the cost of selling the old one (1.8% of its price, an assumption) and about £2,500 of moving costs (an assumption). The premium is assumed to grow with house prices, 0.65% a year faster than inflation (OBR forecast). Not counted: the risk that the premium falls, and the cost of moving out again once school is over (which would add the selling costs and stamp duty of another move).*

Even in London and counting the stamp duty, a move made only for the school (£57k-£73k) costs 31%-40% of London private day fees (£184k). A family that would have moved anyway pays far less.

### 7.2 Grammar school

With £3,000 of tutoring and a one-in-four chance of a place, a lifetime grammar-school pay advantage of 2.3% (boys) or 3.1% (girls) would repay the tutoring. With £8,000 of tutoring and a one-in-ten chance at a super-selective school, it would take 16.5% or 21.0%. On the central (zero) estimate, the tutoring is simply lost; on the most generous one, a boy in the middle of the earnings ranking gains +£9k.

### 7.3 For the country

A private place saves the state about £49k of school spending over seven years (IFS figure for secondary pupils, including sixth forms). The fees come to £125k without VAT, which is left out because it is a payment to the Treasury rather than a cost to the country. So the net cost of a private place to the country is about £75k. Whether the pay advantage is a real gain to the country or just moves good jobs from one pupil to another is a separate question. In one survey, education, type of job and industry together account for nearly all of the men's private-school pay advantage, but that survey has no early test scores (Green, Henseke and Vignoles 2017).

## 8. Limitations

- **Differences no survey measures.** The like-for-like comparisons allow for family background and early test scores, but families who pay for school may still differ in motivation or contacts. That would make the estimates too high rather than too low, but the data cannot show it.
- **Old evidence.** Every study of adult pay is of people who were at school between the 1960s and 2000s. Fees after inflation are now several times what their parents paid, and today's private-school exam advantage is smaller.
- **Pay only.** Pensions, self-employment and investment income, health, happiness and the school years themselves are not counted. Self-employment matters most at the top, where private-school pupils are more common.
- **Simplifications.** Assuming each child keeps the same place in the earnings ranking, reading today's pay at each age as one person's career, and a flat employment rate are simplifications. The three scenarios are meant to cover the range of the evidence, not to put a precise range on the answer.
- **Not yet used.** The large studies that followed everyone born in one week of 1958 and of 1970, the study of a large sample of children born in 2000-02, and the Next Steps study, could be re-analysed directly: comparing on early test scores, checking for false effects, and comparing private, grammar and comprehensive pupils in one model. Their licence does not allow AI-assisted analysis without permission, which has been requested; until then, published results are used.
