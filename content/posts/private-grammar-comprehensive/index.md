*English private, grammar and comprehensive schools compared, raw and adjusted for the entrance filter*

## Abstract

Do private and grammar schools change what their pupils go on to achieve, or do they mostly pick pupils who would have done well anyway? And at today's fees, do private schools pay? I compared English private (independent), grammar and comprehensive schools on university entry, A levels and earnings, first raw and then adjusted for the "entrance filter": the family background and early ability that decide who gets in. The comparison uses public administrative data (DfE, Cambridge, ONS), 25 published studies (303 of 312 estimates checked word for word against the papers), the 2026/27 tax system and 2025/26 fees. It also profiles the top 30 private and top 30 grammar schools.

**Headline: on the best current evidence, private day school from 11 to 18 probably does not pay for itself in lifetime earnings for a typical private pupil. At the 3.5% real discount rate of HM Treasury's Green Book the net present value is −£53k for a boy and −£68k for a girl, a real return of 1.8% and 1.3% a year on the fees. The central estimate is uncertain: at the top of its 95% interval a typical boy just breaks even (+£7k); a typical girl still does not (−£18k).**

- **Raw gaps are large, and grammar schools now match private schools on most of them.** By 19, 88.8% of grammar sixth-formers and 86.9% of private ones had entered university, against 77.2% in other state schools. Grammar sixth-formers are now 10%-24% more likely than private ones to be accepted by Cambridge per A-level student (Figure 1).
- **About half or more of the earnings gap is who gets in.** Private pupils earn 1.45× what state pupils not on free school meals (non-FSM) earn at 30 at the median and 2.12× at the 99th percentile. Adjusted for family background, the most recent estimate (people born in 1989-90, no early-ability measure) is a premium of about 17%, with a 95% interval of 5%-31%; for the 1970 cohort, adjusted also for early ability, it was up to 35% for men. For grammar schools, the estimates for pupils near the pass mark are about zero.
- **The fees set a high bar.** 11-18 day fees cost £149k in present value at age 11. Repaying them needs a lifetime pay premium of about 27% for a typical boy and 33% for a typical girl, well above the 17% central estimate. At 3.5%, only boys who would be top-10% earners anyway (NPV +£18k), a premium as large as the 1970 cohort's (NPV +£7k for a typical boy) or the top of the central estimate's interval (+£7k) come out ahead.
- **Undiscounted, the same boy gains £291k after tax over a career against £165k of fees.** The fees come first and the gains decades later; that timing, not the size of the gain, is what turns the answer negative.
- **Pupils at top private schools beat their GCSE-based prediction at A level; at top grammars, barely.** At the median top-30 private school pupils gain +0.21 of a grade per A-level entry above what their GCSEs predict (the 87th percentile of comprehensives); at the median top-30 grammar, +0.05 (the 62nd). Within the top 30 there is no detectable link between fees and value added (Spearman −0.11).
- **Grammar schools are a much smaller bet.** With £3,000 of tutoring and a one-in-four chance of a place, a pay premium of 2.3% repays the tutoring; the evidence for any premium is weak, so the central expected loss is the tutoring (−£3k).

These are averages for typical pupils and say nothing about a particular child or school. The adjusted estimates come from older cohorts and cannot rule out that some of what remains is still who gets in. Earnings are only one reason families choose a school.

## 1. What is compared, and how

### 1.1 Three kinds of comparison

Every number below carries one of three labels:

- **Raw.** The plain difference between pupils at each kind of school. This mixes what the school does with who it admits.
- **Adjusted for intake (before entry).** Compares pupils with similar family background and ability measured *before* they entered the school (test scores at 7, 10 or 11). This is the answer to "adjusted for the entrance filter". It is still not a proof of cause: parents who choose private school may differ in ways no survey measures.
- **Adjusted for later attainment.** Also holds GCSE or A-level results, degree or university fixed. Because those are partly results of the school, this removes some of the school's own effect. It shows what is left over beyond grades.

"Comprehensive" means a non-selective state school outside the areas that still have grammar schools. Non-selective schools inside those areas (the modern equivalent of secondary moderns) and the 24 schools that select some of their places by ability ("partially selective", such as Dame Alice Owen's and the Watford grammars) are kept separate. England only.

## 2. University and A levels

### 2.1 University entry

By 19, 86.9% of private-school A-level students had entered higher education, 88.8% of grammar sixth-formers and 77.2% of those at other state schools and colleges (DfE widening-participation statistics, pupils aged 15 in 2020/21). Private sixth-formers lead on high-tariff universities (58.6% against 50.2% and 24.2%). These rates have barely moved in 15 cohorts. They describe the school attended at 17, so "grammar" here means grammar sixth forms, which also admit pupils at 16.

### 2.2 Cambridge

[![Line chart of Cambridge acceptances per 1,000 A-level students, English schools, 2013-2023: grammar rises from 23.5 to 26.2; independent falls from 29.1-35.7 to 21.1-23.8; other state about 5.](fig-cambridge.svg)](fig-cambridge.svg)

*Figure 1. Cambridge acceptances per 1,000 A-level students in each kind of English sixth form. The private band runs from Home-fee applicants only (lower edge) to all fee statuses (upper edge), because it is not known whether DfE's private-school counts include overseas boarders. The grammar line subtracts every Northern Ireland state-school acceptance, so it is a lower bound. (click for full size)*

In the 2013 admissions cycle, private sixth-formers were the most likely to get into Cambridge per A-level student. By 2023 grammar sixth-formers were 10%-24% more likely (26.2 against 21.1-23.8 per 1,000); the lines crossed between 2019 and 2022, depending on which private-school fee statuses are counted. Among those who applied in 2025, 31.7% of grammar applicants received an offer, against 25.9% of private applicants and 22.7% from comprehensives. None of this adjusts for who applies, so it is not a school effect; what it shows is that grammar sixth forms have overtaken private ones at Cambridge.

### 2.3 A levels: results and value added

[![Range chart of A-level value added by sector, 2024/25: independent median +0.21, grammar +0.01, partially selective −0.04, comprehensive −0.02, non-selective in grammar areas −0.14.](fig-va-sectors.svg)](fig-va-sectors.svg)

*Figure 2. A-level value added by school, 2024/25: how many grades per A-level entry pupils gained above what their GCSEs predicted. Line P10-P90 of schools, bar P25-P75, tick median. (click for full size)*

On raw results, private and grammar sixth forms are close: 42.9 and 41.6 points per A-level entry (both about a B+), against 33.9 in comprehensives. Value added, which compares each pupil's A levels with what their GCSEs predicted, separates them. Private sixth forms add +0.20 of a grade per entry on average; grammar sixth forms +0.01; comprehensives −0.02; non-selective schools in grammar areas −0.12 (averages weighted by entries; Figure 2 shows school medians).

Two cautions. Value added covers the sixth form only, so it says nothing about ages 11-16. And many private schools sit IGCSEs, which do not count as a baseline, so some private pupils are missing from the measure (below 70% coverage at 53 private schools, but at only 1 of the top 30).

For ages 11-16, Progress 8 compares GCSEs with tests at 11. Grammar schools score +0.67, comprehensives 0.00 and non-selective schools in grammar areas −0.19. Private schools have no Progress 8. Grammar schools select on more than the KS2 tests that Progress 8 controls for (their own 11+ test, tutoring, motivation), so +0.67 is an upper bound on what the schools add; a study of twins that also controls for a DNA-based score finds the grammar GCSE advantage shrinks to 0.03 of a standard deviation (Smith-Woolley et al. 2018).

## 4. Earnings: raw and adjusted

### 4.1 The raw gap is largest at the top

[![Line chart of the ratio of private to state non-FSM pupils' earnings at 30 by percentile: 1.25 at P10, 1.45 at P50, 1.61 at P90, 2.12 at P99; within the same GCSE band the ratio is 1.18-1.39.](fig-ons-ratio.svg)](fig-ons-ratio.svg)

*Figure 5. Earnings at 30 (PAYE, ONS 2022, people aged 30 in 2017-2019) of private-school pupils relative to state non-FSM pupils, percentile by percentile. Grey band: the same comparison within GCSE attainment bands (means). (click for full size)*

At 30, the median private-school pupil earns 1.45× the median state non-FSM pupil. The ratio is roughly flat between the 25th and 75th percentiles (1.48× and 1.48×) and rises only in the top quarter: 1.61× at the 90th percentile and 2.12× at the 99th (£181k against £85k). Within the same GCSE band the ratio of mean earnings is 1.18-1.39×, but that comparison holds GCSEs fixed and so removes part of what the school does. ONS does not split state schools into grammar and comprehensive.

### 4.2 What is left after the entrance filter

[![Forest plot of pay premiums by study, raw and adjusted for background and early ability (the 1989-90 cohort for background only): 1958 cohort 7% (men against comprehensives 25%); 1970 cohort men 20% at 34 and 35% at 42, women 28% and 21%; 1989-90 cohort 17% at 25; graduates 10% men and 5% women; grammar 9%, 7%, −3% and 8%.](fig-evidence.svg)](fig-evidence.svg)

*Figure 6. Pay premiums by study, raw (hollow) and adjusted for family background and ability before entry (filled, with 95% intervals where published). Graduates: adjusted also for A levels, degree and university. (click for full size)*

In every study, adjusting for background and early ability removes a large part of the raw private-school gap: between 37% and 71% of it, measured in log points (Figure 6). For the 1970 birth cohort, the adjusted premium for men was 20% at 34 and 35% at 42; for women it was 28% at 34 and 21% at 42 (Green, Machin, Murphy and Zhu 2012; Green, Henseke and Vignoles 2017). For men in the 1958 cohort it was only 8% against all state schools, but 25% against comprehensives (Dearden, Ferri and Meghir 2002). The 2012 study's working paper does not say at what age school type was recorded, and a later paper co-authored by Green describes it as private primary schooling, so its age-10/11 test controls may partly reflect the school itself. The most recent estimate, for people born in 1989-90 who were at private school at 13, is 17% in weekly pay at 25, adjusted for family background but not for early ability (Green, Anders, Henderson and Henseke 2020). Adding early test scores barely moved the 1970-cohort estimates. For children born in 2000-02, though, adding demographics and test scores at age 3 moves the private GCSE edge from +0.05 to −0.12 of a grade (Anders et al. 2024; none of these is statistically significant), so the 1989-90 figure, which has no early-ability control, may overstate the premium.

Three findings matter for today's pupils:

- **The academic route to the premium may have closed.** For children born in 2000-02 the private-school GCSE advantage is +0.05 of a grade once family background is controlled, about zero (Anders et al. 2024). For the 1970 cohort, about half the rise in the premium ran through better qualifications.
- **The sixth-form route is still open.** Among A-level students born in 1989-90, a private sixth form was associated with a 9 percentage point higher chance of entering university, and among those who went, a 10 point higher chance of a Russell Group place, for pupils with the same GCSEs and background (Henderson et al. 2020); this study's own value-added figures point the same way.
- **What remains after qualifications is smaller.** Among 2006/07 graduates, private-school graduates earned 9.5% more (men) and 4.7% (women) 3.5 years on, with the same A levels, degree class, university and subject (Crawford and Vignoles 2014).

For grammar schools, the studies that compare pupils just above and just below the pass mark find about zero: −3% (men) and +8% (women, imprecise) in hourly pay (imputed from occupation) in Aberdeen (Clark and Del Bono 2016; −6% and +17% in annual income, which also reflects hours), and +7% with a very wide interval in the 1958 cohort (Pastore and Jones 2023). Estimates that adjust by regression are larger (9% for men in the 1958 cohort, Dearden, Ferri and Meghir 2002), but the same methods find a "grammar effect" on test scores *before* pupils reach grammar school (Manning and Pischke 2006), so they overstate it. The pass-mark studies compare grammar pupils with secondary moderns; Dearden et al. compare them with comprehensives in the mixed system of 1974. No study yet estimates the earnings effect for anyone who sat the 11+ after the 1990s, although tax records for them now exist. For a family in a selective area today the alternative is the local non-selective school (value added −0.12, Progress 8 −0.19), close to the old secondary-modern comparison; for a family in a comprehensive area the pass-mark (secondary-modern) estimates transfer less well.

### 4.3 Premium scenarios for today's pupils

The cost-benefit calculation uses three scenarios, chosen to bracket the evidence rather than to pick a single number:

| Scenario | Private 11-18 | Grammar 11-18 | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | rising from 17% at 25 to 35% (men) or 28% (women; the higher of the 1970 cohort's estimates at 34 and 42) | 9.0% (men), 7.7% (women) | private: 1970 cohort unchanged; grammar: men a regression estimate (an upper bound), women a pass-mark estimate (imprecise) |
| Central | 17%, flat | 0 | 1989-90 cohort at 25; grammar: pass-mark comparisons |
| Low | 9.5% (men), 4.7% (women) | 0 | only what remains after qualifications |

The central estimate has a 95% interval of 5%-31%, and results are reported at both ends. The central case assumes the 1970 cohort's rise in the premium with age is offset by the smaller academic advantage of today's private pupils. The low case is not a floor: a premium of zero is not ruled out.

For the sixth form alone (private only at 16-18) the range is 0 to 6.3%. Its central 1.7% prices one channel only (more university entry) and likely understates the channel it measures; the high case is the whole education channel of the 1989-90 cohort.

## 5. Does it pay?

### 5.1 How the calculation works

Pay for a child at a given point in the earnings distribution, ages 22-67, is taken from the 2025 earnings survey (ASHE) by sex and age, for all employees, weighted by the chance of being in work (89.5% of male and 85.9% of female graduates, DfE). Real pay grows 1.7% a year. The premium raises gross pay by the scenario's percentage; the gain is taken after income tax, National Insurance and student-loan repayments, with tax thresholds frozen until 2031 as announced. Everything is in 2025/26 prices and discounted to age 11. Fees are the national average for day schools including VAT (ISC census 2026, 2025/26 fees), £149k in present value for ages 11-18; scaled by ISC's regional fee ratios, about £184k in London and £109k in the North West. Fees are held constant in real terms and extras (lunches, trips, uniforms) are left out; both favour the school. Bursaries and sibling discounts are also left out, which works against the school (a 10% discount is one bar in Figure 8). Discounting at 3.5% for 30 years and 3.0% after is the Green Book convention used by the IFS; 2.1% is roughly the real cost of a mortgage.

The child's position matters because a percentage premium is worth more to a high earner. A typical private pupil would, had they gone to a state school, earn at about the 64th percentile of the distribution: the median private pupil's earnings at 30, less the premium, placed among state pupils (ONS). The calculation assumes the child keeps that position whichever school they attend. ONS does not split this by sex, so in the central case the same position is used for boys and girls (in the high and low cases it differs only because the premium does); and it is a position among people with any PAYE earnings at 30, reused as a position in the ASHE distribution of employees' pay, so the mapping is approximate. It also assumes that school type changes neither hours nor the chance of being in work, that the pupil goes to university on either path (so takes out the same student loan; repayments rise with pay), and that the employment rate for graduates aged 16-64 holds to 67.

The private-school estimates describe, roughly, pupils who actually went private. A family deciding at the margin, for example one priced in or out by VAT, may gain more or less; the evidence cannot say which. (The grammar pass-mark estimates, by contrast, already describe pupils near the cut-off.)

### 5.2 The break-even premium

[![Break-even premium by the child's earnings rank, men and women, at 3.5% and 2.1%, with the premium scenarios: at the median, 31% (men) and 38% (women) at 3.5%; the central scenario is 17%.](fig-breakeven.svg)](fig-breakeven.svg)

*Figure 7. The pay premium needed to repay 11-18 day fees (black), against the premium scenarios for today's pupils (blue). (click for full size)*

To repay the fees, a child at the median needs a lifetime pay premium of 31% (boys) or 38% (girls) at 3.5%, or 21% and 27% at 2.1%. At the typical private pupil's position the hurdle is about 27% for boys and 33% for girls; at the 90th percentile it falls to 15% and 23%. Girls face a higher hurdle because they are more often out of work or part-time, so a percentage premium adds up to less.

| Boy | high | +£7k | +£87k | 3.5% | £524k |
| | **central** | **−£53k** | −£12k | **1.8%** | £291k |

| Girl | high | −£35k | +£17k | 2.5% | £358k |
| | **central** | **−£68k** | −£34k | **1.3%** | £247k |

At the ends of the central estimate's 95% interval the NPV at 3.5% is −£117k to +£7k for a boy and −£123k to −£18k for a girl.*

In the central case the real return on the fees is 1.8% for boys and 1.3% for girls, below even the cost of a mortgage. Over a career the boy gains £291k after tax, well above the £165k of fees, but the fees are paid in the child's teens and the gain arrives over the next five decades. At top-30 school fees the NPV falls by a further £45k (−£99k for a boy).

Private sixth form only (16-18, £48k of fees, valued at 16) needs a premium of 8% (boys) or 11% (girls) at the median. For a typical pupil (about the 74th percentile, since the premium is small) the NPV at 16 is −£36k and −£38k in the central case and −£7k and −£12k in the high case.

### 5.4 What moves the answer

[![Tornado chart of the NPV for a typical boy and girl: the premium scenario moves it most; for boys the child's earnings rank comes next, for girls the discount rate; fees and other inputs less.](fig-tornado.svg)](fig-tornado.svg)

*Figure 8. Each bar changes one input with the others at the central case. Earnings rank: where the child would sit in the earnings distribution on the state-school path. (click for full size)*

For a typical boy the answer turns first on which premium applies to today's pupils and where he would otherwise sit in the earnings distribution, then on the discount rate and the fees. Only the 1970-cohort premium (NPV +£7k), a top-10% earnings position (+£18k) or the top of the central estimate's interval (+£7k) turns his NPV positive; a 2.1% discount rate brings it close (−£12k). A top-30 school's fees take it to −£99k, and a 10% bursary or sibling discount lifts it to −£38k. For a typical girl the premium scenario and the discount rate matter most, and no single change turns the NPV positive.

### 5.5 The cheaper alternatives

- **Moving house.** Buying into a good state school's catchment ties up the extra house price, which comes back when the house is sold; the cost is holding it. Holding a premium of 10-20% of the average English home for seven years costs £7k-£14k in present value for a family that would move anyway, and £20k-£27k if the school is the only reason to move (stamp duty, sale and moving costs included), against £149k for private fees.
- **Grammar school.** With £3,000 of tutoring and a one-in-four chance of a place, a grammar premium of 2.3% (boys) or 3.1% (girls) would repay the tutoring; with £8,000 and a one-in-ten chance at a super-selective school, 16.5% or 21.0%. On the central (zero) estimate, the tutoring is the loss; on the most generous one, a boy at the median gains +£9k.
- **For the country.** A private place saves the state about £49k of school spending over seven years (IFS spending per secondary pupil, including sixth forms). Excluding VAT, which is a transfer to the Treasury rather than a cost, the fees come to £125k excluding VAT, so the net resource cost of a private place is about £75k. Whether the earnings premium is a gain to society or a transfer between pupils is a separate question; in one survey, education, job characteristics and industry together account for nearly all of the male premium, but that survey has no early-ability controls (Green, Henseke and Vignoles 2017).

## 6. Limitations

- **Selection on what is not measured.** The adjusted estimates hold background and early ability fixed; families who pay for school may still differ in motivation or contacts. They are more likely to overstate the school's own effect than to understate it, but that cannot be shown with these data.
- **Old cohorts.** Every adult-earnings estimate comes from people at school between the 1960s and 2000s. Real fees are now several times what those cohorts' parents paid, and today's private-school academic advantage is smaller.
- **Earnings only.** Pensions, self-employment and investment income, health, happiness and the years at school themselves are not counted. Self-employment matters most at the top, where private-school pupils are over-represented.
- **Averages.** Assuming the child keeps the same earnings position, reading today's age profile of pay as one person's career, and a flat employment rate are simplifications; the scenarios are meant to bracket the evidence, not to give a confidence interval.
- **Not yet used.** The 1958, 1970 and 2000 birth cohorts and Next Steps can be re-analysed directly (balancing on early test scores, placebo tests, a three-way private, grammar and comprehensive model). The UK Data Service licence does not allow AI-assisted analysis without permission, which has been requested; until then, published estimates are used.
